Bench Press: Keeping Glutes Down (No Butt Lift)
Problem pattern

Some lifters struggle to keep their hips/glutes in contact with the bench during the bench press. Limb length and bench setup can influence difficulty, but the most common cause is incorrect leg-drive intent and timing, not anatomy.

Common causes
1) Leg drive pushing “up”

If you push through the heels and drive vertically, the force path encourages the hips to rise.

2) Leg drive used only during the press

If leg drive is “off” during the descent and only turns on during the concentric, tension changes abruptly and the body loses stable contact points (often causing hip movement).

Fix 1: Maintain constant leg-drive tension

Leg drive should be present through the entire rep:

On the way down: maintain steady pressure (moderate effort)

During the press: increase pressure slightly (not maximal)

A useful intensity idea:

~50% effort through most of the rep

~75% effort during the press
Avoid 100%: the goal is not to “launch” the bar using legs, but to maintain whole-body tightness.

Fix 2: Drive back into the bench, not up off the bench

Leg drive should create horizontal backward intent:

Think “slide your body backward on the pad”

Your upper traps and upper back are pinned into the bench, so backward drive increases upper-back pressure into the pad

That increased contact helps transfer force to the bar while keeping the hips down

Leg drive does not directly move the bar; it creates tension and stability so the torso/upper back can press efficiently.

Practical cue

Try to push your feet forward and slightly outward, as if driving the pinky toe area toward the front corner of the shoe.
If done correctly:

the body wants to slide backward

glutes stay down because the force is mostly horizontal, not vertical

Bench Press Touch Point: Efficient Descent and Bar Path
Why touch point matters

An inefficient descent often includes:

excessive horizontal bar movement during the eccentric

a low touch point on the torso

This combination forces extra work on the concentric because the lifter must press the bar up and back to finish over the shoulders. In extreme cases, the bar may drift too far back at lockout due to excessive horizontal correction.

“Best” touch point: general guideline

Touch point depends on build and grip width, but a practical rule:

The higher you can touch (while keeping shoulders depressed and elbows stacked under wrists), the less horizontal distance you typically need on the way up.

Clarifying a common cue

The cue “touch at the highest point of your body” can be misapplied.

What you want:

ribcage elevated so the upper sternum / lower chest becomes the high point

Common mistake:

making the lower sternum or upper stomach the high point, which usually leads to an overly low touch point.

Efficient descent model

A clean bench descent is often close to vertical when:

the start position places the bar already aligned over the intended touch point

the bar does not “search” for the touch point during the eccentric

This improves consistency and reduces wasted horizontal movement.

The “J-Curve”: How Much Horizontal Movement Is Ideal?
Basic idea

Many lifters press with a bar path that moves slightly back and up (J-curve). This can be effective and is common among strong benchers.

Efficiency principle

In general:

more horizontal movement = more work

bench press is still primarily a vertical press

A useful target is a small J (or “half J”):

minimal horizontal drift during the descent

only as much back-and-up movement as needed to reach efficient lockout

clean finish over the shoulders without excessive back drift
