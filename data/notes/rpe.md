RPE in Strength Training: A Practical Guide
Concept: What RPE measures

RPE (Rating of Perceived Exertion) is a way to describe how hard a set was, based on your perceived effort. In strength training, it’s mainly used to keep training effort aligned with what your body can actually deliver on that day (sleep, stress, soreness, fatigue, nutrition, etc.).

Key use: autoregulation — adjusting load or reps so the set lands in a target effort zone.

Where the idea came from

A numeric perceived-effort scale became popular through Gunnar Borg’s work in the 1970s, originally in endurance settings. People can generally rate effort in a way that tracks physiological strain (e.g., heart rate) reasonably well.

Why that mattered: it proved subjective effort can be measured reliably enough to guide training.

Why the original Borg scale can fail for lifting sets

Borg-style descriptors (e.g., “very hard,” “extremely hard”) are broad and influenced by what you compare them to.

In resistance training, especially near-failure sets, lifters often underrate how close they were to true failure, because:

“extremely hard” might feel more like a brutal sprint than a hard set of 10

strength work is intense, but short, and doesn’t always feel like “maximal suffering” even when you’re at your rep limit

Result: set-by-set lifting decisions can be messy if the anchor is vague descriptors instead of something lifters can judge more concretely.

The lifting-friendly fix: RPE anchored to Reps in Reserve

The most common strength-training approach ties RPE to RIR (reps in reserve):

RIR = how many additional clean reps you believe you could have performed before technique would break down or the rep would fail.

This gives RPE a concrete anchor: “How many did I have left?”

RPE / RIR quick reference

Use this as a practical lookup (strength-training version):

RPE 10 → 0 RIR (no reps left; another rep would fail or form collapses)

RPE 9.5 → 0 RIR, but you likely had a tiny bit more load available for a single rep

RPE 9 → 1 RIR

RPE 8.5 → 1–2 RIR

RPE 8 → 2 RIR

RPE 7.5 → 2–3 RIR

RPE 7 → 3 RIR

RPE 5–6 → ~4–6 RIR

RPE 1–4 → very easy work (warm-ups / technique practice)

Rule of thumb: RPE becomes easier to estimate when you have enough training history to recognize true grind reps and true failure.

Programming variables: where RPE fits

Strength programs are usually shaped by three big knobs:

Volume

Total work performed (e.g., sets, reps, or tonnage = sets × reps × load).
More volume often helps hypertrophy up to a point, but returns diminish and recovery becomes the limiter.

Frequency

How often you train a lift or muscle group per week.
Often helps by distributing volume so each session stays high quality.

Intensity

Two meanings:

Load intensity: % of 1RM

Effort intensity: how hard the set is → RPE lives here

Important: %1RM alone doesn’t capture difficulty.
The same load can feel very different depending on reps, fatigue, and day-to-day readiness.

Why %1RM and “today’s strength” don’t always match

Your current performance fluctuates with:

sleep quality

stress

hydration/food

accumulated fatigue

soreness

recent training density

So “85% of last month’s 1RM” might be:

a comfortable set today, or

a grind today

RPE lets you keep the effort target consistent even when the day isn’t consistent.

Strength vs hypertrophy: effort targets (practical ranges)
Strength-focused work

As lifters become more advanced, they usually need more training exposure to heavier loads to keep progressing (often 80%+ ranges), while still managing fatigue.

Hypertrophy-focused work

A wide range of loads can build muscle, but very low loads tend to require very high effort to be effective.
Practical takeaway: hypertrophy sets usually need to be close-ish to failure to count as a strong stimulus (commonly somewhere around RPE 5–10, depending on the lift and the goal).

Fatigue management: why “failure every set” is usually a trap

Going to failure can work, but it often creates more fatigue than stimulus, especially on compound lifts.

Simple pattern (same load, different strategy):

If you hit failure early, later sets drop off hard because you’re too fatigued.

If you stop slightly short, you often get more high-quality reps across the session and recover better.

Practical guideline:

compounds: often stay around 1–3 reps in reserve most of the time

isolations: failure is more acceptable (and safer), usually late in the session

How to start using RPE without making it complicated
If you’re new to lifting

Don’t run your whole training by RPE immediately.
Do this instead:

follow a normal plan

log RPE after sets as practice

learn what true technical failure and true grind reps feel like

After consistent training time (months), RPE becomes much more reliable.

If you’re experienced but new to RPE

Run a calibration phase:

for a few weeks, keep your plan unchanged

after each work set, estimate: “How many clean reps did I have left?”

record it next to the set

Example logging format:

5×5 @ 130kg — RPE: 6, 7, 7, 8, 8

Over time you’ll get better at picking the right first-set load to land in your target zone.

Load selection rule that works well in practice

When a workout gives an RPE range (e.g., 7–9):

choose a load that puts your first set near the low end

keep the load stable across sets

allow RPE to drift upward as fatigue accumulates

If RPE jumps too quickly:

reduce load on later sets to stay in the target zone with clean form

Testing guidance (general)

How often you “test” depends on goals and risk tolerance:

sport powerlifting tends to need more specificity

non-competitive training often benefits from safer indicators (rep PRs, estimated 1RM, AMRAPs with good form)

True max attempts are higher risk and aren’t mandatory unless your sport demands them.
