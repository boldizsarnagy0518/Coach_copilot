Sumo or Conventional: Which Deadlift Is Right For Me?

A lot of people ask: How do I know if I should pull sumo or conventional?
And honestly…the frustrating answer is that there isn’t a perfect rule.

Everyone wants a simple checklist—like “long legs = conventional” and “long torso = sumo”—but I haven’t seen a clean, reliable correlation. If there’s any trend, it’s that lighter weight classes (especially women) often lean sumo, while heavier classes often lean conventional. That’s probably just leverages: it’s usually easier for a 52kg lifter to get into a strong sumo position than it is for a 120kg+ heavyweight.

So how do you choose? Here’s what I actually look at as a coach:

1) Which one lets you train pain-free?

This is the #1 factor. I’m personally stronger sumo, but it wrecks my hips—after a couple sessions I’m in enough pain that I can’t keep training it. For some lifters it’s the opposite. If one stance consistently causes pain or irritation, that makes the decision easy. Staying healthy matters more than being “built” for a style.

2) Training age: if you’re new, start conventional

If someone is brand new to deadlifting and wants to get into powerlifting, I usually start them with conventional. It’s simpler to teach, there’s a bit less going on technically, and the hinge pattern is a fundamental movement that everyone should be able to learn. That doesn’t mean they’ll be conventional forever—just that it’s smart to master the basics first before experimenting with sumo.

3) Weight class trend is real—and I use it as a clue

This isn’t a hard rule, but it’s useful. If I have a lighter weight class lifter pulling conventional and their deadlift is noticeably weak compared to their squat/bench, that’s often a sign they might be better suited to sumo.

Generally, lower weight class lifters can deadlift a lot relative to their squat (unless they have a major mechanical disadvantage). As weight classes go up, squat and deadlift tend to get closer—and in heavyweights, squat often even surpasses deadlift. So if a lower-to-mid weight class lifter is barely deadlifting more than they squat, I’ll usually have them test sumo.

4) What looks natural matters (the “coach’s eye”)

This is subjective, but important. Leverages do matter—and sometimes one stance just clearly fits better.

Example: a long torso means a longer moment arm from shoulders to hips, which usually increases demand on the back. For that lifter, being more upright is often helpful. Which stance typically allows a more upright torso? Sumo.

That said, it’s not one-size-fits-all. You can’t turn this into a strict “if X then Y” rule. But in practice, the stance that looks and feels most natural is often the safest and least stressful option.

5) Choose the stance that lets you hit the best positions

Mobility and structure matter. If someone has poor hip mobility (either structural or just tight), sumo might be a struggle because they can’t get into a good start position.

On the flip side, if someone has very short arms, conventional can feel more like a squat than a deadlift because they have to sit so low to reach the bar. In that case, sumo’s shorter range of motion can help them find better positions.

6) Your squat stance can be a strong hint

This one actually correlates pretty well in my experience. Wide-stance squatters often pull sumo better, and narrower squatters often pull conventional better.

Two reasons:

If you can squat wide comfortably, you probably have the hip mobility for sumo.

Strength tends to carry over: wide-stance squatting and sumo deadlifting share more similarities, so transfer is usually higher.

7) If you can’t “push the floor away” in conventional, try sumo

Some lifters never really figure out how to use their legs off the floor in conventional. That cue—leg pressing the floor away—can be harder in conventional because there’s naturally less knee bend and more hip bend.

I’ve seen lifters struggle for months to “find” leg drive in conventional, then switch to sumo and suddenly their quads light up and their deadlift jumps fast. Not because sumo is magically stronger, but because it puts them into a position where they can create better tension and mechanics.

8) After all that…pick the one that’s stronger (as long as it doesn’t hurt)

Once you’ve checked pain, positions, and movement quality, it usually comes down to strength. Some people are just clearly stronger in one stance, and if it’s pain-free, that’s the obvious choice.

Most people who debate this are pulling conventional and thinking about trying sumo. My general rule of thumb:

If your sumo is within ~90% of your conventional right away, there’s a good chance it’ll become equal or stronger once you train it consistently.

If it’s even closer than that, it often makes sense to switch sooner.

If it’s well below 90%, it could still go either way—but usually that gap comes from poor positioning, mobility limitations, or pain making sumo feel unnatural.

Bottom line: there isn’t a perfect formula. It’s a judgment call based on pain, positioning, and performance—but hopefully this gives you a practical way to think about it.

The Most Common Individual Difference in Deadlift Programming

Programming varies a lot person to person, but deadlift is where I see the biggest differences. In general, most people respond better to lower intensity and lower volume on deadlift compared to squat and bench.

Even within that, I usually see lifters fall into one of three buckets:

1) The “middle of the road” majority (about 70%)

These lifters do well with fairly standard deadlift programming:

about 2x/week (one main day + one variation day)

reasonable volume

most work around 70–85% and roughly 4–8 RPE

with a lot of the work living in the 5–7 RPE range

2) The “volume-tolerant, intensity-hating” group (about 20%)

These lifters can handle plenty of work, but they can’t handle heavy pulling. They do best with:

most work in the 2–5 RPE range

around 65–75%

higher overall volume, but low relative intensity

And for them, it’s not as simple as “just drop volume and go heavier.” Heavy work—whether absolute or relative—just beats them up. I’m in this category myself (and one of my athletes is too). One heavy deadlift week can genuinely make our next week’s 1RM worse by 40–50 lb.

3) The “intensity-tolerant, volume-hating” group (about 10%)

This group is the opposite: volume crushes them, but heavy singles and doubles make them feel great.

The common prototype I see here is short-armed lifters who pull through a bigger range of motion. That makes sense—true workload isn’t just sets and reps, it’s also distance traveled. For a short-armed lifter, a set of five is more total work than it is for someone with long arms.

What to do with this

If your deadlift is stalled and you’re not sure why, look at your programming and ask if you’re treating yourself like the wrong category. In my experience, I’ve basically never seen someone thrive on both high volume and high intensity in the deadlift—most people fit somewhere in these three lanes.
